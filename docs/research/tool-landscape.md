# Tool landscape

Research date: 2026-09-07

## Method and rating caveat

This is a qualitative desk review of publicly reachable/indexed pages and official descriptions. It covers six menopause tools, six sleep/temperature tools, and five designed health decision-support tools. We inspected what each tool says it asks or shows, registration and privacy statements where visible, content/search organization, and the likely mobile burden. We did not create accounts, submit sensitive answers, buy products, inspect authenticated screens, run network interception, or run a standardized lab-performance suite against third-party sites.

Accordingly:

- **Observed** means visible on the live/indexed page or in official documentation.
- **Not established** means the public sample did not support a confident answer.
- “Mobile at night” is a design heuristic from the visible flow/content, not a completed device usability test.
- Product-bias and medical-claim-risk ratings describe structural risk, not intent or legal compliance.
- “Performance” is limited to crawlability and apparent page weight/interaction model unless explicitly stated; no third-party Core Web Vitals score is claimed.

Sources and URLs are recorded in [source-register.md](./source-register.md).

### Menopause-tool observations

- **Best pattern:** ACOG and NHS Inform turn observation into a better clinician conversation without claiming a diagnosis.
- **Useful but wrong product model:** MRS tools deliver a concise score, but this project must return a pattern explanation and action—not a symptom burden score.
- **Avoid:** “Could this be menopause?” assessments, probability/likelihood language, and broad personal-history collection.
- **Opportunity:** Existing trackers ask what symptoms occurred. One Change Tonight can ask the much narrower environmental questions needed to decide what to change tonight, then export only a concise factual record.

### Sleep-tool observations

- **Fastest pattern:** one screen of inputs followed by an immediately legible result.
- **Common weakness:** most tools begin with what to buy, budget, or sleep product preference. None of the sampled product finders first asks whether the whole room, the bed microclimate, a sudden personal episode, damp recovery, or only one side is the real unresolved problem.
- **Content pattern:** publishers support quizzes with many search landing pages and strong internal linking, but commercial pages often collapse “cooling” mechanisms and embed ads or product calls to action.
- **Opportunity:** match the speed and card clarity while returning a free experiment, explicit constants, and uncertainty rather than a SKU.

## Product decisions from the benchmark

1. Keep the current one-question card pattern and explicit Continue button; it suits a tired user better than auto-advance.
2. Put “why this matters” beside each question, following MyHealthfinder's rationale pattern.
3. Return an action plan, not a score. Scores invite false precision and medical interpretation.
4. Keep answers local and say so before question one, near saving/check-in controls, and on deletion.
5. Use the ACOG-style clinician-preparation bridge: observable facts, onset/change, frequency, and impact—not a suggested diagnosis or test list.
6. Borrow NCI's candor about exclusions and model limits, but not numeric clinical-risk output.
7. Do not reproduce product-quiz logic. Budget and subscriptions belong only after the environmental constraint and premium fit have been established.
8. Publish a visible evidence taxonomy: authoritative health guidance, peer-reviewed environmental mechanism evidence, manufacturer specification, editorial inference, and unknown.
